Army · 68W

Combat Medic Specialist

A combat medic is not just an EMT. The role covers emergency and trauma care, patient assessment, medication administration under protocol, medical documentation, medical logistics, and clinic support, often while leading and training others. That range supports many clinical, logistics, public-health, and healthcare-administration directions, though civilian roles still require their own licensure.

Your rank changes the story

Use the band that reflects what you actually did—not rank alone. An E-2 and an E-7 in the same code should not submit the same résumé.

01

E-1–E-4

Lead with hands-on patient care, procedures performed, certifications (e.g., NREMT/TCCC), and aid-station or line-medic settings.

02

E-5–E-6 / NCO

Add section-medic leadership: junior medics trained, clinic/treatment-team supervision, protocols enforced, and readiness tracking.

03

E-7–E-9 / Senior NCO

Emphasize medical operations planning, personnel and equipment management, program oversight, and advising commanders on medical readiness.

EMT / Paramedic

Field trauma care and composure map directly to emergency medical services.

Build evidence, not just a translated title

Hands-on scope

List systems, tools, vehicles, environments, certifications, and recurring tasks.

Leadership scope

State people trained or supervised, asset value, work volume, readiness, safety, and quality outcomes.

Credential gap

Identify licenses, degrees, software, or civilian standards required before claiming the target role.

Search wider

Search by capabilities and equipment as well as by “68W” or “Combat Medic Specialist.”

Reality check: MOS, AFSC, rating, or rank never guarantees civilian qualification. Duties vary by unit and assignment; regulated professions require their own education, experience, exams, or licenses.
