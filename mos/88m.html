Motor Transport Operator

Motor transport is more than driving a truck. It covers convoy planning and execution, load planning and cargo securement, vehicle inspection and fault reporting, hazardous-materials handling, and movement tracking and accountability, often leading teams and managing fleets. That opens driving, logistics, fleet-management, safety, and government directions.

Your rank changes the story

Use the level that reflects what you actually did—not rank alone. Two people with the same code and very different experience should not submit the same résumé.

E-1–E-4

Junior Enlisted

Lead with vehicles operated, license classes, cargo/HazMat handled, PMCS performed, and the environments you drove in. Do not inflate supervisory scope.

E-5–E-6

Non-Commissioned Officer

Add convoy or dispatch leadership: drivers led, movement planning, safety and licensing oversight, and the assets you managed.

E-7–E-9

Senior NCO

Emphasize movement control, multi-team operations, maintenance and safety programs, and resource/readiness forecasting.

Turn 88M into proof employers trust

A translated title isn't enough. These four moves turn Motor Transport Operator experience into evidence a civilian hiring manager can act on.

  1. Show what you ran

    List the systems, vehicles, equipment, and tools you personally operated as Motor Transport Operator—plus qualifications, certifications, and the recurring high-stakes tasks you owned.

  2. Put numbers on leadership

    Soldiers led, dollar value of equipment you were accountable for, workload, readiness rates, and safety or quality outcomes. Scope beats adjectives.

  3. Close the credential gap early

    For any target role, name the license, degree, or software it requires and start now—your GI Bill and credentialing assistance can cover most of it.

  4. Search by capability, not code

    Recruiters rarely search “88M.” Search job boards by the equipment, skills, and outcomes above as much as by “Motor Transport Operator.”

How to use this: Treat these as targets, not limits. Your exact duties depend on your assignments, so lead with what you actually did—and remember that regulated careers require their own license, certification, or degree.
